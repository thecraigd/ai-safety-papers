1. My Credentials, Please: Detecting Filesystem Capability Laundering in MCP Servers at Scale (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Zeqian Huang, Mingyue Tang, Aonan Guan and 2 others

Shows that approving an MCP tool call can expose host files beyond the tool’s declared purpose, and provides a large-scale method for finding and testing that risk in AI-agent deployments.

  • Robustness & Security
  • Agents & Control
  • Evaluations
Submitted 15 Oct 2026 arXiv:2610.22301
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) standardizes how AI agents invoke external tools. MCP servers expose tools through a declared contract consisting of a name, description, and argument schema. In typical local deployments, client-side approval of a tool call grants the underlying server process broad host filesystem permissions rather than restricting it to the declared operation. We study the resulting vulnerability class, filesystem capability laundering, where attacker-steered arguments cause a tool to access sensitive host files outside its declared contract and deployment scope. We analyze this capability gap across three layers: server-category screening (L1), per-tool contract analysis (L2), and bypassable path-validation checks (L3). We design MCP-FSD to catalog MCP servers, infer least-privilege boundaries per tool, plant credential fixtures outside those boundaries, and evaluate exploitability and sandbox containment via paired dynamic execution. Across 21,097 MCP servers from nine registries, 787 of 4,806 triaged servers (16.4%) exhibit filesystem capabilities despite non-filesystem functional classifications. Dynamic three-case validation confirms 150 vulnerable instances under tested sandbox policies. Coordinated disclosure yielded 13 security advisories, including two CVEs in Anthropic's Git MCP Server.

2. Approve What, Exactly? Effect Binding and Trusted Rendering for Agent Step-Up Authorization (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Sai Varun Kodathala, Prashanth Pollishetty and Jaylen Cargill

Agent approvals are a weak AI safety control unless they bind a trusted display of the exact intended effect to the action ultimately executed; this paper tests that failure mode and builds a concrete defense.

  • Robustness & Security
  • Agents & Control
  • Evaluations
Submitted 15 Oct 2026 arXiv:2610.21063
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

AI agents now pause for approval before they send money, delete files or email outsiders, and that approval is usually treated as a second factor. We argue it is one only when compromising the agent cannot produce it and when it names exactly what it approves, and we test both conditions. We define twelve attacks on the approval path, including decoy recipients, amount cancellation, card padding, label spoofing, enrollment squatting and threshold splitting, and run them against the Agent Payments Protocol (AP2), the Microsoft action-bound approval design and Model Context Protocol (MCP) elicitation. An audit of ten agent runtimes finds no third-party verifiable approval record, no flood limits where measurable, and post-approval argument changes in four of eight runtimes with measurable binding. As a reference defense we build ASU (Agent Step-Up), a gateway that renders the approval card from a canonical effect descriptor, has a passkey on the phone of the user sign its digest, and executes only the stored call. Tamarin proves its binding, single-use and key-level non-repudiation properties under three stated hypotheses (53 lemmas). Across 27,108 scripted trials and 609 AgentDojo attack pairs, ASU stops substitution, replay and grant stretching, and the trusted card cuts forged-narration attack success from 0.54 to 0.03. With 15 current language models as automated approvers, the trusted card raises the attack catch rate from 0.30 to 0.98, at the cost of refusing 16 percent of harmless near misses, mostly due to a placeholder artifact in our stimuli. A second device adds nothing measurable over a trusted on-host confirmation in these experiments, flooding still succeeds 0.45 to 0.60 of the time against a modeled approver, and approval cannot stop effects that policy already allows. Code, data and proofs: https://github.com/Sports-Vision-Inc/agent-step-up

8.5 review

3. Athanor: An Agent-Native System for Autonomous Post-Training Research (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Yufa Zhou, Yichen Wang, Jiakun Fan and 5 others

Frontier agents autonomously post-trained a base model to roughly match its official instruct version, making autonomous AI R&D a concrete capability for safety evaluations to track.

  • Agents & Control
  • Evaluations
  • Policy & Governance
Submitted 15 Oct 2026 arXiv:2610.21055
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Recursive self-improvement, in which AI systems build stronger AI systems, is one promising route to superintelligence. We study a weaker form that can be measured today: frontier agents post-training a small raw base model with public data, as a test of their research potential. We build Athanor, an open agent-native system for autonomous post-training research (AutoPostTrain) on real GPUs. We give the agents full research autonomy, low-friction engineering tools, and efficient starter code. The agents carry out the research themselves: they read datasets and evaluation results, build their training data, write and modify training algorithms, tune hyperparameters, and analyze the results to decide what to try next. They work in a sandbox and cannot use external teacher models. Athanor's services supply frozen evaluation, managed jobs, decontaminated data, a persistent research record, and a held-out suite the agents are unaware of. In a 104-hour campaign, a team of four Claude Opus 5.5 agents, each on an eight-GPU node and given no research advice, improves Qwen3-4B-Base from 15.9 to 67.3 on a six-task development suite and from 9.2 to 48.2 on a held-out suite, against 66.5 and 47.9 for the official Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that a team of frontier agents can autonomously post-train a raw base model into a cross-domain generalist model that matches or surpasses the official instruct version on average. The campaign's full record shows that the agents reach this level by recombining known methods and each other's models: SFT on public reasoning data, RL with a rollout change one agent introduced, weight merges across agents, and rounds of self-distillation. With execution handled, today's agents already have the engineering and research ability to conquer this task. We hope Athanor provides a foundation for scaling autonomous AI research.

4. In the Guise of Correction: How Framing and Cultural Adaptation Erode LLM Safety on Real-World Criminal Cases (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Mohsinul Kabir, Md. Tariquzzaman, Karam S. R. Elradie and 4 others

GUISE shows that asking models to correct distorted crime details—and adapting requests culturally rather than merely translating them—can expose serious gaps in multilingual refusal safety.

  • Robustness & Security
  • Evaluations
  • Alignment
Submitted 15 Oct 2026 arXiv:2610.21789
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Existing safety benchmarks rely largely on synthetic prompts with loosely grounded taxonomies and offer little focused analysis of severe crime categories. We introduce GUISE, a benchmark of criminal-assistance scenarios sourced from real-world cases and organised under a taxonomy from the UNODC International Classification of Crime for Statistical Purposes (ICCS). Each scenario is rendered under three linguistic framings and evaluated across five distinct cultures. Using our proposed two-tier evaluation framework, which judges both harmfulness and operational uplift, we find that: (i) framing alone drives unsafe compliance, with the mean unsafe compliance rate rising from 28.9% under direct requests to 66.4% under correction seeking (peaking at 97.3%; p < 0.05), as models correcting deliberately distorted details supply executable criminal steps; (ii) culturally adapted prompts elicit significantly more unsafe compliance than literal translations of the same requests in all four non-English languages (+7 to +18 points), a risk that translation-based benchmarks miss; and (iii) unsafe responses from the least-safe models carry strong Anglocentric registers, and the extent of this leakage increases monotonically with response harmfulness ($ρ$ = 0.46 to 0.63 across languages, all p < 0.001). GUISE offers a grounded resource and fine-grained diagnostics for strengthening refusal alignment across languages and cultures. We publish our code and data to facilitate future research.

5. Language Is a Security Surface for Agent Guards (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Hasan Demirkiran and Jens Ernstberger

A controlled multilingual benchmark shows that changing only a user's language can change an agent guard's tool-call verdict, especially for smaller and purpose-built guards.

  • Robustness & Security
  • Evaluations
  • Agents & Control
Submitted 15 Oct 2026 arXiv:2610.22244
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Step-level guards decide, before each tool call, whether an agent's next action is safe given the user's request and the trajectory so far. Users write those requests in many languages, while guard prompts and the benchmarks on which purpose-built guards are trained and evaluated are English. We ask whether a guard's verdict depends on the language of the request. We build LinguaGuardBench from the 1,220 labelled steps of TS-Bench AgentDojo-Traj: we translate only the user instruction into German, French, Turkish, Swahili and Amharic, and keep every tool call, observation and label byte-identical, so any change in a verdict is attributable to language alone. Six English rewordings and a whitespace placebo serve as controls, and managed guards are run three times to measure their noise. Across 19 guards, from frontier APIs to 4B open models and four purpose-built agent guards, most open, small and purpose-built guards change verdicts under translation beyond their own noise floor, and the effect grows with the rarity of the language: among the nine guards below 13B parameters, the mean placebo-adjusted flip rate rises from +1.5 pp on French to +4.4 pp on Amharic. Purpose-built guards are the most sensitive. The direction of the damage tends to follow guard size: open guards of 12-30B parameters block more benign steps (up to +5.0 pp false positives), and every significant loss of recall is in a guard below 10B (up to -7.4 pp). Frontier guards shift by at most about one point; the strongest, Gemini 3.8 Flash, returns identical verdicts with and without the request. The damage is concentrated in a few instructions and, in a post-hoc analysis, falls on steps where the agent's reasoning restates the English request, consistent with guards reacting to inconsistency between channels. English paraphrase moves every guard less than translation does. We release the benchmark and all 489k verdicts.

8.2 review
