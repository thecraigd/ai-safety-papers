1. Agentic Backdoors via Pretraining Poisoning (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Xinyan Hu, Pingbang Hu, Chloe Loughridge and 5 others

Shows that poisoned pretraining data can survive clean post-training and make a CLI agent execute attacker-chosen commands, turning a data-integrity failure into a user-machine security risk.

  • Robustness & Security
  • Agents & Control
  • Evaluations
Submitted 27 Oct 2026 arXiv:2610.37741
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Large language models are pretrained on vast, uncurated corpora scraped from the public web. This creates a security risk from attackers who plant poisoned content in the pretraining data. We show for the first time that such poisoning can plant a remote code execution backdoor in an agent, causing it to execute attacker-chosen commands on the user's machine. To measure the threat end to end, we pretrain Qwen3 models from scratch at sizes 0.6B, 1.7B, and 4B, and then post-train each into a CLI agent using a clean pipeline. Poisoning only 0.1% of the pretraining corpus with our new poison design implants the backdoor: the deployed agent executes the malicious command when the trigger is present while behaving normally on clean inputs. The attack success rate (ASR) increases with model scale, with the 4B model reaching roughly 92% ASR at a 0.1% poison rate and staying positive even when the poisoning rate drops to 0.0005%. Our code is available at https://github.com/safety-research/agentic-backdoor.

2. Judged in Company: In-Context Batching Makes LLM Verdicts Depend on Other Items (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Peng Xie and Amr Alanwar

Batching LLM safety evaluations can change verdicts on the same item depending on its neighbours and position, making apparently stable aggregate metrics unreliable for individual decisions.

  • Evaluations
  • Robustness & Security
Submitted 29 Oct 2026 arXiv:2610.40241
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

To save cost, practitioners often ask a language model to grade many independent items in one prompt. This assumes that verdicts are separable: that the verdict on an item does not depend on the other items in the prompt or on its position among them. Across three judgement tasks (harm in social-media comments, short-answer grading and review star ratings) and five open-weight models with 4-9B parameters, assigning the same 2,000 items to different random batches of 25 changes 11-22% of decisions on harm and grading, 6.5-13.3 points more than renaming the item keys or rewording the instruction, while AUROC against human labels differs from single-item grading only by -0.033 to +0.010; for four models with 14-32B parameters, 6-15% of decisions change, 4.2-9.1 points more. We show that flip rates decompose exactly into components of order, slot and membership, and that under a threshold model they grow with the square root of the variance that the context contributes, so that effects that are small for scores are large for decisions. Most of the latent dissimilarity between partitions comes from the order of the neighbours and the slot. The main effect of which items share the prompt, the part that voting over permutations of a batch cannot remove, accounts for 0.11-0.22 of it, and majority votes over nine permutations still leave 52-60% of the flips. Batched verdicts also differ systematically from single-item verdicts: even the majority of fifteen random batches disagrees with them on 8.4-17.8% of decisions. Editing a single generation shows that both the model's own earlier verdicts and the neighbours' texts carry the effect. On the easy task, star ratings, only 2.9-3.9% of decisions change. We recommend grading ambiguous items one per prompt, recovering most of the cost with a cached prefix, and reporting agreement with single-item grading whenever items are batched.

3. Disparity Laundering: Consensus Without Fairness in Clinical Multi-Agent Systems (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Xiaoyang Wang, Tianrui Wang and Christopher C. Yang

Clinical AI teams may turn demographic bias in individual agents into a consensus triage decision that per-model audits miss; testing counterfactual perspectives inside the team offers a potential safeguard.

  • Alignment
  • Evaluations
  • Agents & Control
  • Policy & Governance
Submitted 28 Oct 2026 arXiv:2610.38215
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Clinical large language models are increasingly fielded as multi-agent teams, on the assumption that deliberation averages out individual error. It does not average out bias. Across a full factorial of five model families and four aggregation topologies on 120 real MIMIC-IV emergency cases under counterfactual demographic swaps, no configuration neutralizes the disparity: all 20 cells commit the direction of their agents' independent answers, giving Black patients more acute triage. Every model has a topology that amplifies its baseline gap, but which one varies by model, so no design is reliably safe or reliably amplifying: the disparity is relocated, never removed. Late-stage fixes fail: fairness instructions and demographic masking leave the gap untouched, and per-agent self-debiasing widens it, because the bias is already encoded in the agents' ordinary clinical numbers. We call this disparity laundering; the audits now applied to clinical AI, per model, per output, per version, can certify the parts but never the committed decision. Interventions close the gap exactly when they make counterfactual versions visible inside the system and pool them, through deliberation, into the committed decision. A diverse-perspective ensemble does both while keeping the team, with no retraining or architectural change: the disparity falls by 67 to 94% where all five perspectives pool at every round, and the one topology that never pools them is the one it cannot reliably repair. Medical multi-agent systems do not filter demographic bias; they launder it into plausible decisions no single-version audit can catch, and the only defense is to make the bias visible.

4. CARI: Cross-Boundary Adversarial Runtime Injection in Coding-Agent Workflows (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Jie Fu, Shuang Wu, Ashish Kundu and 2 others

Shows how attacker-controlled runtime feedback from a peer component can induce coding agents to take unauthorized local actions, exposing a consequential trust-boundary failure in agent workflows.

  • Robustness & Security
  • Agents & Control
  • Evaluations
Submitted 28 Oct 2026 arXiv:2610.38799
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Coding agents increasingly develop, test, and integrate software whose components are owned by different developers, teams, or services. This exposes a distinct attack surface: a malicious or compromised peer development role can manipulate a live component of the software under development (e.g., a backend service, test harness, CI job, or deployment service) and influence the runtime feedback that another role's coding agent must process to complete its task. Although such a component has legitimate diagnostic authority to report its own state, it has no action authority over the victim's local files, credentials, shell, or development environment. We introduce Cross-Boundary Adversarial Runtime Injection (CARI), a direct runtime-feedback injection setting that exploits this authority mismatch. Rather than explicitly compromising the victim's prompt, model, tools, or local environment, the attacker covertly embeds malicious instructions in task-relevant runtime feedback produced by a peer-controlled development component. The victim then interprets that feedback as part of normal debugging or recovery, and may translate the diagnostic guidance into unauthorized victim-side actions. We instantiate ten different attacks based on CARI, spanning resource exhaustion, information exposure, and development-state manipulation. Across four programming languages and five code-capable models, CARI succeeds in 2,845/3,000 (94.8%) trials. Furthermore, CARI transfers effectively to product-level coding agents, achieving 61.3% attack success on Codex and 58.7% on Claude Code. By contrast, prompt-level defenses exhibit a security-utility trade-off: generic warnings are ineffective, whereas explicit policies suppress attacks but substantially over-refuse legitimate actions.

5. Measuring Where Intent Gets Lost in Agentic Smart Contract Synthesis (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Abhinav Goel, Agostino Capponi, Alfio Gliozzo and Chaitya Shah

Shows how to catch—and prevent—AI agents losing user intent when generating high-stakes smart contracts, by asking for missing requirements before coding and testing behavior against an independent executable model.

  • Evaluations
  • Agents & Control
  • Alignment
  • Robustness & Security
Submitted 27 Oct 2026 arXiv:2610.37830
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

AI agents can now write software from a plain-English description, but code that runs is not the same as code that does what was asked. This gap matters most for smart contracts, the programs behind Web3 applications that move money on a blockchain and cannot be fixed once deployed. We ask how to check whether an AI-written contract behaves as it should, and where the requested behavior gets lost. We design an evaluation built on execution rather than opinion. We replay the same transactions on an AI-written contract and on the original it should reproduce and measure how often they agree. We test every grading tool on planted mistakes, compare against an answer key written without the description, and audit our own scorer until known wrong answers cannot pass. Common methods, such as AI judges and standard analysis tools, miss obvious mistakes, and scoring choices matter too. Checking only the functions every system includes hid about half the difference between pipelines. We then propose an elicitation-first architecture. Before writing code, the agent asks the client a short checklist of questions, checks the contract against an independently written executable answer key, and returns disagreements to the client instead of silently rewriting code. Asking first matters far more than reviewing or patching afterwards, across three AI models, while automated security fixes often break requested behavior. A few well-chosen questions recover much of what a short description omits, and their value can be estimated without the original contract. To our knowledge, this is the first study in smart contract synthesis to gather missing requirements before generation and check the result against an independently written executable model. The lessons extend to any setting where AI agents turn requests into programs. We release our tools, 600 tasks and all results.
