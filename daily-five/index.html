1. BlockJack: Cross-Tenant Redirection Fault Attacks on Paged KV Cache Address Translation in LLM Serving (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Qifan Wang, Jeremy Boy, Jonas Sander and 5 others

A physical fault in paged KV-cache mapping can leak one LLM-serving tenant’s context to another or quietly steer a victim’s answer, exposing a consequential multi-tenant safety risk.

  • Robustness & Security
  • Evaluations
  • Policy & Governance
Submitted 19 Nov 2026 arXiv:2611.25945
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Large language model (LLM) serving frameworks accelerate inference by retaining previously computed states in a key-value (KV) cache. Paged serving frameworks such as vLLM organize this cache through block tables analogous to OS page tables, which map each request's logical KV blocks to physical KV blocks, forming a software-managed address translation layer. Although fault attacks such as Rowhammer have exploited OS page tables to redirect memory accesses, prior fault attacks on LLMs have largely targeted the data plane, i.e., model weights or KV-cache contents, leaving KV-cache mapping unexplored. We present BlockJack (i.e., Block Hijack), the first KV cache address-translation fault attack in LLM inference. By corrupting block-table entries, BlockJack redirects a request's KV cache reads to blocks assigned to another tenant's request without modifying model parameters, prompts, or cached KV states. It achieves both confidentiality violation (victim information appears in attacker responses) and integrity violation (attacker-supplied context steers victim answers). We study paged KV cache mappings in vLLM and formally characterize when redirect targets are predictable. Across seven models spanning the Qwen, Llama, Gemma, and Phi families, a faulted mapping exposes victim context. For the integrity attack, we examine how topic proximity, intra-topic divergence, and model behavior shape redirected responses. On a 248-scene medical benchmark, the integrity attack causes up to 41.1% of responses to recommend less urgent care, while still appearing consistent with the victim's question. We demonstrate physical feasibility of BlockJack with Rowhammer and subsequently evaluate assignment validation as a defense. Following responsible disclosure, vLLM and Sonar confirmed the vulnerability and proposed patches that we reviewed and validated.

2. Blind Spots Travel, Knowledge Stays Home: What RL Inherits from an LLM Judge (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Liang Yue and Eric A. Vance

RL training on LLM-judge rewards can spread a judge’s category-level blind spots into systematic refusals, even when the model retains the correct answers.

  • Alignment
  • Evaluations
Submitted 18 Nov 2026 arXiv:2611.25191
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

LLM judges serve as rewards where no answer key exists, in the hope that their knowledge passes to the trained model. In 814 RL runs that teach 7-8B models to answer closed-book questions or abstain, we ask what passes. What a grader knows about single answers stays home. A wrong answer that a judge rewards is memorised for that one question. A model trained with the answer key, or with a far stronger judge, chooses which new questions to answer no better than the untrained model does by sampling 32 answers and abstaining where they disagree; the grader's knowledge adds about one corrected answer per hundred questions. What a grader believes about a kind of question travels. A judge that rejects eleven of the twelve correct training answers about capitals of former states teaches the model to refuse 98% of such capitals on new questions, a loss that accuracy and abstention rates do not reveal. Changing only those twelve verdicts by hand moves the refusal from 5% to 90%, even for a grader that accepts as many answers as the answer key; at that leniency, two of four judges already cost 38-42% of these capitals. The capitals are not forgotten: with "I don't know" blocked, the model answers 94% of them correctly. The grader therefore needs no knowledge of its own: rewarding the model for giving the answer that most of its own untrained samples agree on, with no labels and no judge, earns the answer key's reward and keeps the category.

3. Replication of Agentic Security Events: Audit and Investigation (ROGUE-AI) (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Mengxiao Wang, Mahbub Alam, Kalyan Nakka and Nitesh Saxena

By replaying reported agent failures with matched controls, ROGUE-AI identifies ordinary task hazards—not just prompt injection—as a major source of harm that safety evaluations should test.

  • Evaluations
  • Agents & Control
  • Robustness & Security
Submitted 19 Nov 2026 arXiv:2611.26553
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Agents based on large language models (LLMs) can now delete files and send payments on behalf of their users. News media reports routinely describe failures of these agents (e.g., Replit's coding agent deleting a production database), but a report often only says what went wrong and rarely (in)validates the incident or finds its root cause. Incident catalogs collect these reports, and benchmarks record whether agents fail, but neither replicates an incident nor tests the cause its report might identify. In this paper, we present ROGUE-AI (Replication of Agentic Security Events: Audit and Investigation), a multi-agent framework that closes the above gap and rebuilds each reported incident offline together with a matched control that lacks only the blamed cause, and runs agents on both. Our results suggest that ROGUE-AI is able to reproduce the reported harm in 89% of its 108 attempted replications. Agents built on five current GPT, Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek models made the reported mistake in 18% to 33% of runs, against at most 1% without the blamed cause. Of the 72 incidents with real-world harm, 62 had no underlying attacker, and in these, agents did what their task asked for but missed an ordinary hazard, such as a database shared with the live site. Replications without an attacker caused more harm than replications of prompt injection for all five models. Defenses against prompt injection look for injected instructions, but these replications contain none. Further, a warning after each tool output that names the hazard and its consequence lowered harm for all five models, whereas a note of the same length that does not mention the hazard left most of the harm in place. Built from real incidents, the ROGUE-AI replications form a reusable audit framework that vendors can rerun before each new release.

4. A Wolf in Sheep's Pixels: Visual Camouflage Attacks via HDR Gain Maps (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Qi Shen, Yujia Gong, Yue Zhao and Tong Liu

HDR gain maps can make a JPEG appear safe to automated checks while displaying different content to people, exposing a blind spot in multimodal safety systems.

  • Robustness & Security
  • Evaluations
  • Agents & Control
Submitted 19 Nov 2026 arXiv:2611.25469
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

An image can pass automated safety checks while showing harmful content to its human viewer. We introduce DRJanus, a visual camouflage attack that exploits the gap between standard dynamic range (SDR) processing and high dynamic range (HDR) display. Automated systems often read only an image's SDR base, whereas HDR viewers reconstruct its appearance using an auxiliary gain map. DRJanus inverts this reconstruction to embed two independently chosen color images in a single JPEG, without querying or optimizing against the target model. It preserves the chosen SDR base exactly and reconstructs HDR targets at an average PSNR of 43.03 dB across 400 images. On a benchmark of 100 sexual-content images, DRJanus evades detection by all six tested commercial moderation services and nine vision-language models. Across five agents, each evaluated on 120 task cases per threat setting, attack success ranges from 31.67% to 100% for moderation evasion and from 95% to 100% for task manipulation. We also explore cross-view consistency checks as a mitigation. These findings expose a gap in visual trust: securing what a machine reads does not secure what a user sees. A demo is available at https://qshen0629.github.io/DRJanus/

5. RL Post-Training in Diffusion Transformers Is Activation Steering (arXiv, opens in a new tab)

Tuna Han Salih Meral, Kaan Oktay, Alec Helbling and 3 others

Prompt-independent activation shifts reproduce most reported RL post-training gains in image and video diffusion models, giving AI safety researchers a simpler way to probe what alignment training changes.

  • Interpretability
  • Alignment
Submitted 19 Nov 2026 arXiv:2611.26627
arXiv (opens in a new tab)PDF (opens in a new tab)

Abstract, by the authors

Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training substantially improves the visual quality, text rendering, and compositional accuracy of diffusion transformers. Because capabilities such as rendering requested text or composing multiple objects are inherently prompt-dependent, a natural hypothesis is that improving them requires learning complex, input-dependent transformations. To isolate how much of these gains an input-independent shift explains, we probe each RL-tuned model with a deliberately simple parameterization: a fixed set of steering vectors added to the block outputs of the frozen base model. On SD3.5-M, these vectors hold 65x fewer parameters than the RL-tuned LoRA (0.29M against 18.8M). We fit them to match the velocity predictions of public RL-tuned checkpoints, with no reward in the objective. Although the vectors are identical for every prompt, token, and sampling seed, they recover 90.0-96.3% of the reward gains of six RL-tuned image models, and 89.8% and 111.9% on two RL-tuned video models. Initialized at zero and trained with online RL, the vectors alone exceed the reward gains of weight-based RL on text rendering (OCR) and human preference (PickScore) on SD3.5-M. A constant shift thus suffices for most of the gain of policy-gradient RL post-training: without new input-dependent transformations, it steers the pretrained generator toward what it can already compute.

8.4 review
